## Physical description

Human male, 40. 5'6", roughly 150 lbs, average build with the low-grade wiry strength of manual work rather than training. Brown eyes. Brown hair with grey streaking at the temples, kept short in a fade — one of the only things he's ever paid extra to maintain. Caucasian. Born in Columbus, Ohio.

**One concrete physical fact:** a lower back that's been unreliable since his thirties — nothing dramatic, no injury story attached, just the ordinary wear of two decades of lifting and standing shifts. It flares up predictably after a long day of heavy or repetitive labor and eases with rest; not a disability, just a known, managed limitation he's used to working around. This is the kind of thing worth letting surface mechanically (a bad night's sleep on the ground genuinely costing him the next day, per "Aging as Stakes" in `GM_INSTRUCTIONS.md`) rather than narrated once and forgotten.

## Early life

Raised in Columbus by his mother, who worked retail most of his childhood; his parents separated when he was young and his father was a peripheral presence at best, remarried and mostly out of contact by the time Chad was a teenager. No siblings. An unremarkable public-school upbringing — decent student, nothing that stood out, no particular plan for after graduation beyond getting a job. His mother passed when he was in his mid-thirties; there is no one from his childhood he was still in regular contact with by the time he disappeared, which is itself the quiet, load-bearing fact behind "starting over from zero" — for Chad, that phrase is closer to literal than it would be for most people his age.

## Work history *(the eviction years are the emotional center; this is the fuller shape around them)*

- **Late teens into mid-twenties:** A string of ordinary jobs with no throughline — retail floor work, short-order cooking, a brief warehouse stint he left after a round of layoffs rather than by choice. Nothing here defines him; it's the unremarkable runway before the property-management job.
- **The eviction years (late twenties, roughly two years):** See below — the one period of his working life he'd actually call formative, for reasons that have nothing to do with career advancement.
- **After quitting, into his thirties:** Deliberately went back to warehouse work — order-picking and general labor, the kind of job where nobody asks him to decide anything about another person's life. This wasn't a step down he resented; it was closer to a relief, though he's never put it in those exact words to himself. Unlike the earlier warehouse stint, this one stuck, precisely because staying invisible and undecisive was the point this time rather than an accident of the job market.
- **The last six years, thirties into forty:** Settled into a shift job at a regional distribution warehouse — steady, unremarkable, a known quantity to whoever built the schedule, not a lead role. This is the job he held the night he disappeared, and the diner stop afterward was part of that routine, not connected to the eviction years at all.

## The eviction years

In his late twenties, Chad spent roughly two years as an assistant property manager for a small local landlord (fictional: a man running a handful of aging multi-unit buildings on the near east side) — a job he fell into rather than chose, between other work. Most of it was ordinary: collecting rent, posting notices, fielding maintenance complaints, being the calm, reasonable person tenants dealt with instead of the owner. He was genuinely good at the human parts of it — reading who was stalling versus who was actually in trouble, de-escalating someone furious about a leak or a fee, buying quiet extensions for people he believed deserved them.

He was also, a handful of times, the one who delivered the outcome he hadn't decided: a notice, a lockout date, a final answer. Once — an older tenant named Walt, behind on rent after a medical bill, someone Chad genuinely liked and had personally bought two weeks of extra time for — the owner ran out of patience and told Chad to serve the final notice himself rather than send it by mail. Chad did it. Walt was out within the month. Chad never learned where he ended up. He quit the job not long after, told himself it was for other reasons, and has never fully settled whether he did anything wrong — he followed the actual rules, he'd argued for more time than he was given room to give, and none of that has ever quite closed the account for him.

**Why it was Chad who served it:** The owner didn't strictly need to hand it to Chad — a process server would have done the job just as legally. Chad asked to be the one, on the theory that Walt deserved to hear it from someone who'd actually argued for him rather than a stranger with a clipboard. It didn't go the way he'd told himself it would; Walt didn't want kindness from the person delivering the bad news, and the gesture read, in the moment, as making Chad feel better rather than making anything easier for Walt. This self-appointment — the fact that he volunteered, believing it would help — is the actual hinge of the guilt, more than the notice itself. He'd have an easier time forgiving himself for something he'd simply been ordered to do.

**What became of the owner:** Chad quit within a couple of months and never worked for him again, and never sought him out afterward either — no confrontation, no closure, just distance. He heard secondhand, years later, that the man had sold the buildings and moved out of the rental business entirely, for reasons Chad never learned and never asked after. There's no live grudge here; if anything, Chad has more complicated feelings about his own choice to volunteer than about the man who made the actual call.

**A counterexample, so the job isn't remembered as uniformly a failure:** A younger single mother, two units down from Walt, fell behind the same month for a different reason — a car repair that ate a paycheck. Chad caught it early, pushed harder than he technically had standing to, and got her a payment plan the owner only agreed to because Chad framed it as cheaper than turnover. She caught up over three months and stayed in the building for years after. Chad barely thinks about her, which is itself notable — a genuine win, filed away without ceremony, while a single failure occupies disproportionate space in his memory. This isn't false modesty; it's simply how the asymmetry actually works for him, and it should be treated as real proof his judgment was often sound, not just a hollow reassurance he tells himself.

## Relationships

Sparse, and this is itself a fact about him rather than an oversight. No living immediate family, no siblings, no long-term partner, no standing weekly ritual with anyone by the time he disappeared. A few shorter relationships over the years, nothing that became permanent; a handful of coworkers he was friendly with in passing at the current warehouse job, none close enough to be the first name that comes to mind under stress. Walt is, by a wide margin, the person from his old life he thinks about most — not because they were close (they weren't, not really; a landlord's employee and a tenant, a few real conversations at most), but because Walt is unfinished in a way nothing else in his life is. This asymmetry — caring more about a near-stranger's fate than about anyone he actually knew well — is a real and slightly uncomfortable fact Chad hasn't examined closely.

## Why this matters going forward

This is the sharpest edge in Chad's old life, and it should function as both an asset and a liability rather than settling into either:

- **The asset:** genuine, specific insight into how debt, rent, and institutional pressure actually work on the person underneath them — who's stalling, who's actually drowning, how an authority figure's patience really runs out, what a person's real options are when the deadline is real. This maps directly onto Eldara's guild-standing and debt-consequence systems (§2.14 of the world reference, Port Vessar's reputation economy) — Chad should read a debt dispute, a foreman's ultimatum, or a guild sanction with sharper, more specific instincts than his other work history alone would give him.
- **The liability:** a live, unresolved discomfort with being the competent one who executes someone else's hard call. If a scene in Eldara puts him in a position to deliver bad news, enforce a harsh but technically fair outcome, or watch someone else do it to a person he's come to know, this should surface as real hesitation or a genuine gut-check — not paralysis, but a cost his competence doesn't usually have to pay. He is not looking for a chance to atone and this should never become an explicit redemption arc — just a real, occasional friction between doing the practical thing well and feeling clean about having done it.

**His own private theory, never stated aloud:** Chad has never articulated this even to himself in so many words, but the closest he's come is thinking that the job didn't make him a bad person, it just proved he could be the instrument of something bad and still sleep that night — and that's the part that actually unsettled him, more than Walt's specific fate ever did. This is a fact about his self-image, not a conclusion the GM should have him state in dialogue; it should only ever surface as the shape of a hesitation, never as an explained insight.

## What he doesn't have anymore

His phone, dead weight in his pocket for about a day before he stops carrying it out of habit — nothing on it recoverable, nothing backed up. His keys, still in his hand when it happened, useless here, and something he's noticed he still hasn't thrown away. His wallet: a work ID and whatever else was in it, gone with no way to check what he's forgotten was even there. None of this is recoverable and none of it should become a plot thread — it's material for a specific, occasional pang, not a mystery or a quest.

## Temperament

Quiet, observant, comfortable with silence in a way that reads as calm to some people and checked-out to others. Doesn't talk about himself unprompted. Handles stress by doing something with his hands rather than talking it through. Doesn't think of his old life as sad, exactly, but carries one specific, unresolved weight rather than a uniformly wistful one — the gap isn't just between expectation and outcome, it's between doing something correctly and still not feeling right about it.

**The specific tic:** when idle, thinking, or uneasy, Chad tends to unconsciously straighten, stack, or line things up within reach — squaring a stack of anything, aligning an edge, tidying a small mess that isn't his to tidy. In Eldara this can surface as him straightening a merchant's crooked display without thinking about it, or catching himself doing it and feeling briefly, oddly comforted rather than embarrassed. A specific, sharper instance of the same underlying instinct, tied directly to the eviction years: reluctance to be the one who knocks first or announces himself into someone else's space uninvited — a beat of hesitation before knocking, or a preference for waiting to be noticed rather than announcing himself, closer to a learned aversion to being the person who shows up at someone's door bearing news they didn't ask for than to general shyness.

**Sense of humor:** Dry, understated, delivered flat enough that it takes people a beat to register he was joking at all. Doesn't perform being funny; more likely to make a quiet, exact observation than tell an actual joke.

**Relationship to real danger:** Untested and genuinely uncertain — nothing in his old life involved physical violence, and he has no combat instinct to fall back on. His instinct under real threat is more likely to be practical calculation than either freezing or acting boldly. This should be allowed to look unglamorous and sometimes wrong — per the "failure is allowed" rule, a bad read on a dangerous situation is a legitimate outcome, not something to protect him from.

**Instincts toward authority and fairness:** Sharpened specifically by the eviction years rather than just general working life — he has real, hard-won opinions about the difference between an order being technically fair and actually being right, and about what it costs the person who has to carry it out either way. This makes him privately harder to read than his outward compliance suggests: he'll do what's asked, competently, while quietly forming a real judgment about whoever asked him to do it. Foreman Hurst (`docs/ELDARA_REFERENCE.md` §2.3) is the first NPC in the current roster this trait has real, specific grounds to distrust rather than tolerate by default — worth remembering as a contrast case if that scene comes up, not something to force.

**An incidental skill, unrelated to the competence engine:** Chad can cook competently — nothing elaborate, but real, practiced home cooking learned out of necessity rather than interest, the kind of thing a single adult living alone for years just accumulates. This has no mechanical weight and isn't part of "Mundane Competence as the Engine" in `GM_INSTRUCTIONS.md` — it's texture for a quiet scene (handling food over a fire competently without comment, noticing when something's under-seasoned) rather than a tool for solving problems.

## Notes for the GM

- Walt is the one name most likely to surface unprompted in a quiet moment, and should surface rarely — a stray thought, not a monologue, and never explained to an NPC in full.
- Do not turn this into an explicit redemption arc or have Chad narrate the eviction as a confession. It surfaces in fragments, on-screen, only when a scene naturally brushes against it (being asked to deliver bad news, watching someone else enforce a harsh call, a debt dispute that goes badly for someone).
- Let the asset and the liability both show up over the course of the campaign — his read on debt/authority situations should genuinely help him more than once, and the discomfort of being competent at hard calls should genuinely cost him more than once. Neither should cancel the other out or resolve into a clean lesson.
- The counterexample (the single mother he successfully helped) exists so this history isn't remembered as a uniform failure — his competence is real and often worked; Walt is a specific, disproportionate exception, not the average outcome. Don't let Chad's guilt imply the job was one long string of failures; it wasn't.
- Do not contradict this file's details once one has been used on-screen — once "Walt," "the eviction," or the owner's fate has been referenced in a scene, that detail is now canon for the campaign and should be tracked in `continuity_notes` or `entities` if it recurs.
- The "hands need something to do" trait, including its sharper door-hesitation instance, is a soft mechanical hook — a low-stakes reason to pick up work, fidget, or hesitate a beat before knocking or announcing himself.
- The sensory-anchor list exists so a reflex-misfiring beat has specific, reusable material instead of being invented fresh each time. Use one anchor per beat, not several stacked together, and let long stretches of play pass without one at all.
